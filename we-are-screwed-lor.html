Policy critique · Social epistemology

We are screwed lor — but perhaps not quite.

Singapore’s media regulation can protect public knowledge while restricting citizens’ participation in producing it. This site examines the “neutral”, “technocratic” and “well-regulated” institutions that shape what we know, and invites you to question them.

Content block 1

Current research

The January 6, 2021 attack on the US Capitol is a cautionary tale about misinformation, polarisation and the erosion of shared epistemic norms. Yet the failures of a liberal information environment do not justify its opposite: a highly centralised, state-regulated one.

In Singapore, the state has long regulated media, public expression and political participation, justified by social stability, racial and religious harmony, and the prevention of misinformation and foreign interference. These concerns are not without merit. In the 2026 World Press Freedom Index, RSF ranked Singapore 123rd of 180. The question extends beyond factual accuracy to who decides which claims are credible, whose experiences are represented, and which interpretations are recognised.

Hostile epistemology (Nguyen, 2022)

Humans are cognitively finite. We rely on heuristics, networks, credentials and trusted authorities, and these dependencies can be exploited by features of our environment: seductive conspiracy theories, echo chambers of structured distrust, interfaces that reward engagement over accuracy. Such hostility need not be intentional. Whether a given arrangement encourages critical revision or prematurely ends inquiry is something to investigate, not presume.

Hermeneutical injustice (Fricker, 2007; Riegler & Veigl, 2025)

Hermeneutical injustice describes disadvantage in understanding or communicating one’s social experience because of marginalisation in the collective production of interpretive resources. Riegler and Veigl show it can occur even when concepts exist, if a dominant meaning suppresses others. Terms such as racial harmony, meritocracy, pragmatism and family may mean different things to different groups, while some meanings become institutionalised in law, policy and media narratives.

Environmental factors

Social media, recommendation algorithms and generative AI change the scale, speed and cost of misleading content. Echo chambers can dismiss outsiders systematically, so exposure to opposing evidence may not be enough.

Behavioural change or institutional intervention?

Holliday, Lelkes and Westwood (2025) meta-analysed 77 depolarisation treatments across 25 studies: an average gain of 5.4 points on a 101-point scale, typically fading within two weeks. Durable change may need attention to elites, institutions and incentives. The American evidence cannot be directly generalised to Singapore.

Van Bavel et al. (2024) update the identity-based model of belief: beliefs serve social functions such as group membership and loyalty, and informational environments shape which sources and identities become salient. Identity does not necessarily undermine accuracy; professional identities in journalism and science can strengthen verification norms.

Content block 2

“Bad actors” or solutions? Institutions and the paradoxes of paternalism

Preventing manipulation and protecting social cohesion are legitimate concerns. Yet interventions also distribute power over what audiences encounter and whom they regard as credible. Their justification therefore depends on their effects on epistemic agency: citizens’ practical ability to investigate claims, contribute experience and challenge authoritative accounts.

Why protection matters

Under FICA, restrictions targeted 95 social media accounts in July 2024, which the Ministry of Home Affairs attributed to a foreign network posting about Singapore’s leadership transition. Taken as the ministry’s assessment, this shows how apparently separate communications can be organised manipulation.

Why dependence requires scrutiny

POFMA correction directions generally add official clarifications while leaving the original post accessible, though stop-communication directions exist. The Court of Appeal upheld POFMA’s constitutionality in The Online Citizen v Attorney-General [2021]. FICA offers ministerial reconsideration and a Reviewing Tribunal. These make decisions contestable, but can citizens obtain and circulate the evidence needed to challenge them?

Scrutiny depends on how public inquiry is organised

Teo’s interviews with 17 civil society participants identified verification costs, reputational concerns and difficulty obtaining official information. This shows mechanisms, not a population-wide self-censorship rate. Licensing matters too: IMDA suspended The Online Citizen’s class licence in September 2021 over funding-disclosure obligations. Whatever its justification, it affects the continuing availability of spaces for inquiry.

Consequences for shared understanding

Grasswick’s participatory epistemic injustice (an analogy from science) asks whether differently situated people can help set what needs investigating. IMDA’s refusal to classify #LookAtMe cited religious denigration and social division, a substantive concern that also restricts a narrative about homosexuality and religious authority. These cases do not show a blanket prohibition; they show how regulation shapes the interpretations available, which is where Fricker and Pohlhaus (willful hermeneutical ignorance) become relevant.

Criteria for assessing the protective environment

Digital Skills for Life and SG Digital Office workshops address misinformation, but a Singapore pilot (Wong & Wu, 122 participants) found no significant advantage for a misinformation game over its control. REACH offers a feedback channel, but the question is whether participation shapes questions and assumptions or only communicates views inside existing agendas. Protection against manipulation stays legitimate; its justification also depends on access to evidence, meaningful review and opportunities for affected communities to contribute.

Content block 3

Vibrant hermeneutic environments

Censorship and marginalisation can create a subaltern class. As digital and public spaces face more control, how lived experiences are communicated, and whose voices are shared, has material consequences. Examining how narratives are constructed around the most marginalised communities can reveal the latent hostilities of “pragmatic” policies. Riegler and Veigl’s spaces for plurality point to what a better environment might look like.

Content block 4

Conclusion and insights

We inhabit an increasingly complex information environment in which misinformation can arise from malicious actors, technological incentives, institutional practices and our own cognitive limits. As Nguyen reminds us, we cannot escape dependence on imperfect epistemic shortcuts.

Yet recognising vulnerability does not mean surrendering epistemic agency, nor granting any single institution an unquestionable monopoly over public knowledge.

Singapore’s challenge is not simply to prevent false beliefs, but to cultivate an environment in which claims can be scrutinised, mistakes corrected, and diverse experiences made intelligible. A resilient environment needs both reliable institutions and the capacity to question them: shared standards of evidence without uniformity of interpretation.

Perhaps the most pragmatic response to our limits is not to eliminate disagreement, but to build institutions and communities that can learn through it. Majulah!
